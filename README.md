# Demand Forecasting Project

A complete time-series forecasting pipeline for retail demand, comparing 7 different models from naive baselines to gradient-boosted trees.

This project is built for **learning**: every script is heavily commented, and this README explains the supply-chain and statistical concepts behind every decision.

## The Concepts, Explained

### 1. Why demand forecasting is the heart of supply chain

Every downstream decision in supply chain assumes a demand number. Procurement teams place orders based on forecasted demand. Production schedules are built around it. Inventory targets (safety stock, reorder points) are calculated using forecast error. Even network design (where to put warehouses) depends on regional demand patterns.

A small forecast improvement can save enormous cost. Walmart-scale operations save hundreds of millions per percentage point of MAPE improvement, because the savings flow through inventory carrying costs, expediting fees, and stockout losses simultaneously.

### 2. Time-series components

Any time series can be thought of as a combination of:

- **Level** — the baseline value around which the data oscillates
- **Trend** — long-run upward or downward movement
- **Seasonality** — repeating patterns at fixed periods (day-of-week, month-of-year)
- **Cyclicality** — longer non-fixed waves (business cycles, fashion cycles)
- **Noise/Residual** — what's left after explaining the above

We use `seasonal_decompose` from statsmodels to separate these visually. The decomposition plot in `outputs/plots/02_decomposition.png` shows this in action. Look at the seasonal band — perfectly periodic. Look at the trend — clearly rising. The residual contains promo spikes and noise.

### 3. Stationarity and why ARIMA needs it

A stationary series has constant mean, constant variance, and constant autocorrelation over time. Classical ARIMA assumes stationarity. We test this with the **Augmented Dickey-Fuller test**:

- Null hypothesis (H0): the series has a unit root (non-stationary)
- If p-value < 0.05, we reject H0 and call the series stationary

If non-stationary, we **difference** the series (compute `y_t − y_{t-1}`). The number of times you difference is the `d` parameter in ARIMA(p, d, q). Seasonal differencing (`y_t − y_{t-7}` for weekly) handles seasonal non-stationarity.

### 4. ACF and PACF

- **ACF (Autocorrelation)**: correlation of the series with its lagged versions, including indirect effects.
- **PACF (Partial Autocorrelation)**: direct correlation only — controls for intermediate lags.

How to read them for ARIMA order selection:

| Pattern | Suggests |
|---|---|
| ACF tails off, PACF cuts off at lag `p` | AR(`p`) |
| ACF cuts off at lag `q`, PACF tails off | MA(`q`) |
| Both tail off | ARMA / mixed |
| Significant spike at lag 7 | Weekly seasonality → use SARIMA |

In practice, modern workflows use `pmdarima.auto_arima` to grid-search these automatically.

### 5. Train/test split for time series

The golden rule: **never randomly shuffle**. Time has direction; using future to predict past leaks information and gives unrealistically good metrics.

The right way: chronological split. We hold out the **last N days** as the test set. For real production, you'd go further and use **walk-forward validation** (also called expanding-window or rolling-origin), where you make forecasts at each step and re-train as the window advances. That tests the model the way it will actually be used.

### 6. The models

**Naive (`y_hat = last value`)**  
The simplest baseline. If a complex model can't beat this, it's adding no value. In short horizons with stable processes, it's surprisingly hard to beat.

**Seasonal Naive (`y_hat = same value 7 days ago`)**  
Respects weekly seasonality. Strong baseline for retail.

**Moving Average**  
Average of the last N observations. Smooths noise but reacts slowly to changes. Larger N = more smoothing.

**Holt-Winters (Triple Exponential Smoothing)**  
Decomposes into level, trend, and seasonality, each with its own smoothing parameter (`alpha`, `beta`, `gamma`). Smoothing means recent data weighs more than old data, exponentially. Good when patterns are stable; can't easily use external regressors.

**SARIMA**  
Combines AR (autoregression on past values), I (differencing for trend), MA (using past forecast errors), and seasonal versions of each. Parameters `(p, d, q) × (P, D, Q, s)`. Powerful but interpretation requires care; assumes linear relationships.

**Prophet (Meta)**  
Fits an additive model: `y(t) = g(t) + s(t) + h(t) + ε`, where `g` is trend with automatic changepoint detection, `s` is seasonality (Fourier-based), and `h` is holidays. Designed for business time series with multiple seasonalities. Robust to missing data and outliers. Easy to use, often a strong default.

**XGBoost (gradient-boosted trees)**  
Reframes forecasting as a supervised regression problem. We engineer features that are known in advance (day of week, month, day of year, week of year, quarter, weekend flag, promo). The model learns non-linear interactions. Pros: handles external regressors naturally. Cons: needs careful feature engineering and validation to avoid leaks; can't extrapolate trend beyond what it's seen.

### 7. Metrics

| Metric | Formula | When it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **MAE** | mean(\|y − ŷ\|) | Direct, in original units. Good for explaining to non-technical stakeholders. |
| **RMSE** | √mean((y − ŷ)²) | Penalizes large errors more. Use when big misses are especially costly (e.g., stockouts). |
| **MAPE** | mean(\|y − ŷ\|/y) × 100 | Scale-free percentage. Easy to compare across SKUs. Breaks down when actuals are near zero. |
| **Bias (ME)** | mean(ŷ − y) | Are we **systematically** over- or under-forecasting? Hugely important — a model with low MAE but negative bias will silently cause stockouts month after month. |

**Operational consequence of bias**: a negative bias (under-forecasting) means you'll regularly buy less stock than needed → stockouts, lost sales, expedited shipments. A positive bias means excess inventory → carrying cost, obsolescence, markdowns.

## What the Results Tell Us

In the sample run, Prophet won with MAPE ≈ 7.8%. Several models had large negative bias (−15 to −37), meaning they **systematically underforecasted**. Why? The 60-day test window covers November–December — the heaviest Q4 holiday period. Models that don't have explicit yearly-seasonality structure (moving avg, seasonal naive, SARIMA with only weekly seasonality) miss the holiday surge entirely. This is a great real-world lesson:

> **Your model is only as good as the seasonality it captures.** Always match the periodicity in the model to the periodicity in the data.

Prophet won because it explicitly models yearly seasonality by default. XGBoost did well because `month` and `day_of_year` features let it learn the Q4 pattern.

6. **Probabilistic forecasts**: quantile predictions, not just point estimates — directly feeds into safety-stock calculations.
7. **Tie forecasts to inventory decisions**: compute the safety stock and reorder point implied by each model's forecast error distribution, and show the dollar impact.

## Notes on the Synthetic Data

The data generator builds a realistic retail series with:
- Linear trend (+30 units over 3 years)
- Weekly seasonality (weekend peaks)
- Yearly seasonality (Q4 holiday lift)
- Specific holidays (Black Friday, July 4th, pre-Christmas)
- Random promotions (~30 over 3 years, 3-day duration each)
- Gaussian noise

This means we know the "ground truth" patterns — so we can verify that our models actually learn what we expect them to.
